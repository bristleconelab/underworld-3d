      About Underworld

      When two plates meet, the older, heavier ocean floor bends down into the mantle at a trench. It keeps going: the slab sinks a centimetre or two a year, and after 100 million years or more it can reach the bottom of the mantle, almost 2,900 km down. Seismic waves travel a little faster through these cold slabs, so seismologists can map them.

      This globe shows three things at their real depth: the slabs found in the mantle, the plates still diving under trenches today, and the earthquakes that break inside them. The surface is see-through so you can look inside; the depth scale is true.

      Where the data comes from

      • Sunken slabs: van der Meer, van Hinsbergen and Spakman (2018) Atlas of the underworld, Tectonophysics (CC BY 4.0), table as updated on atlas-of-the-underworld.org. A dot marks the middle of each slab; the bar under it spans its top and base; the dashed line joins it to the place where the rocks at the surface record its subduction.
      • Plates diving under trenches: Slab2, Hayes (2018), U.S. Geological Survey (public domain).
      • Earthquakes of magnitude 5 and above since 1973: USGS ComCat (public domain).
      • Surface: NASA Blue Marble through GIBS (public domain).

      What to keep in mind

      Slab positions, depths and ages come from reading tomography images and plate history; each has an error of about 100 km in depth and several million years in age. Seismic tomography images themselves are not shown here because their reuse terms are not stated.
